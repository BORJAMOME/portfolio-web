Sales analysis
Sales report with an IBCS approach
3
IBCS visual types built
5
Data model dimensions

Two periods can close with the same total and have been completely different underneath — without the change built into the visual, you can’t see that difference.

IBCSDAXStar schema modellingDrill-downAbsolute and relative changeDynamic parametersVisual hierarchySales analyticsYoY analysis

The problem

In many sales teams, sales analysis starts and ends with the total. If the figure goes up, all is well. The problem is that two periods can close with the same number and have been completely different underneath: one with real growth, the other with a fall offset by a one-off campaign. Without the change built into the visual, you can’t see that difference.

The goal

To build a sales report that lets you read volume and change in a single view, without switching tabs or comparing separate tables. And to do it following the IBCS standard, which defines how to show that information so that everyone reads it the same way, without ambiguity.

My approach

The IBCS (International Business Communication Standards) standard sets concrete rules for showing change: which colour means a positive variance, which shape means actual versus budget, and how volume and change share the same axis without overlapping or getting mixed up.

I built three types of visual following that standard. The multi-tier column chart shows the monthly absolute value, the absolute change against last year and the percentage change at the same time. The column chart with built-in change packs that information into each bar, which helps when the axis has many items, such as products or categories. The variance line chart shows the trend over time, with the area between the actual line and the target shaded according to whether the target is met.

The model sits on fact_ventas with five dimensions: Product, Campaign, Region, Customer and Calendar. An extra IBCS-Selector-Medidas table handles the dynamic switch between Total Quantity Sold and Total Sales in euros without duplicating pages.

Tools used

Power BI for the model, the three IBCS visual types and navigation between views. DAX and visual calculations together: DAX for the base measures, the dynamic switch between Quantity and Sales in euros, and conditional formatting; visual calculations to generate the CY vs SPLY and against-target changes directly on each visual without multiplying measures in the model.

What I learnt

Replicating the IBCS standard in Power BI without custom visuals forces you to really understand how conditional formatting works at measure level and how to build changes that behave correctly with any combination of active filters. The most demanding part was keeping the three tiers of the multi-tier column chart in the right proportions whatever the value range of the selected filter. I also learnt that following a visual standard doesn’t limit the analysis: it makes it easier to read for anyone who receives it.

Star schema modellingPower QueryDAXSales vs targetsProfitability analysisDrill-downYTDVisual hierarchyRetail analytics

The problem

Leadership had numbers in front of them but no context. They knew the day’s takings but couldn’t say which categories were eroding profit, which store was below average on margin, or whether they were meeting targets or just beating the previous month. Each department looked at its own part. There was no single source connecting sales, profitability and targets. That isn’t a data problem. It’s an analytical structure problem.

The goal

To build a reporting system that makes it possible to go from a variance in a KPI down to the specific receipt that explains it, with sales, margin and targets connected in the same model and no need to cross-check separate spreadsheets.

My approach

The underlying mistake was the order in which the analysis had been approached: partial views on unmodelled data, instead of first designing how the business should work as a model. So that’s where I started. A star schema with two fact tables, Sales and Budgets, connected to the same date, store, product, category and target dimensions. Power Query to transform and standardise the raw point-of-sale data. T_DISC disconnected tables to handle cross-filtering without contaminating the model. TopN parameters for the category and product selectors.

The system has five views: Annual Sales shows overall performance against budget and the YTD trend by store. Store Ranking compares sales, margin and profit by store and category at the same time. Average Ticket analyses buying behaviour by store, product and day. Compare KPIs puts any store side by side with the rest. And from any point in the dashboard you can drill down to the receipt and product that explain the variance.

Key findings

Mahon was the store with the highest sales volume (€277,000) but carried a negative variance of €47,700 against budget. Sant Francesc Xavier, with less volume, was the only one closing in the green, €6,000 above target. By category, Drinks and soft drinks was the best seller at €43,500 and also generated the most margin (+37.58%). Convenience and Express Store closed with a negative margin. Two categories that look similar in volume but behave in opposite ways in profitability.

Tools used

Power Query to transform and clean the point-of-sale data before the model. Power BI for the star schema and the dashboard’s five views, with drill-down to daily receipt level. DAX for the YTD sales measures, performance against budget, margin by category and product, and average ticket with comparison across stores.

What I learnt

Drilling down to receipt level was the most demanding part of the model. For it to work correctly with any combination of active filters, the relationships between tables had to be designed from the start with that granularity in mind. I also learnt that order matters: designing the model before the dashboards isn’t a methodological preference, it’s what determines whether the system holds up or breaks when someone asks a question nobody anticipated.

Social Media Performance | 2025 vs 2024
26,3%
Spend saving — same performance
0,21€
CPC (down from €0.25)

Impressions fell 12.8% against 2024, but spend fell 26.3% — efficiency improved even though volume dropped.

Year-on-year DAXDisconnected tablesCTR · CPCStar schema modellingSparklinesYoY comparisonMarketing analyticsEfficiency vs volume

The problem

The usual problem with marketing reports is that volume is compared without looking at investment, clicks are analysed without looking at CTR, and conversions are celebrated without analysing their cost. KPIs are reviewed in isolation, and that leads to readings that drive the wrong decisions. The challenge wasn’t to add more metrics but to add context without adding noise: to make every figure always appear next to its reference point.

The goal

To build a dashboard that answers not whether the figures go up or down, but whether the investment is performing better or worse than the previous year, with nine key metrics — Impressions, Reach, Engagement, Clicks, CTR, Spend, Conversions, CPC and Conversions € — filtered by platform, post type, campaign and month.

My approach

I designed the model on a star schema with fact_table at the centre and seven dimensions: Calendar, Platform, Content, Campaign, Post type, Target audience and Boosted. The last one makes it possible to filter specifically by boosted vs organic posts, a distinction that completely changes how social media data reads.

One of the least visible but most important pieces was the period selector. Instead of filtering the model directly, I used a disconnected table (MesCortoSelector) that marks the period to analyse. That guarantees the year-on-year calculations stay consistent with any combination of active filters. Every KPI is shown in the same frame: current value, previous year’s value, absolute change, percentage change and a monthly sparkline to spot the trend without changing page.

Key findings

In May 2025 impressions fell 12.8% against 2024 and reach fell 10.1%. At first sight, worse performance. But spend fell 26.3%. CPC went from €0.25 to €0.21. The business was generating similar results while spending considerably less, which means efficiency improved even though volume dropped. That reading isn’t possible if the KPIs are analysed separately. It only appears when investment and performance sit in the same frame of reference.

Tools used

Power BI for the star schema, the nine KPI cards with built-in sparklines and the filters by platform, campaign, post type and period. DAX for all the year-on-year measures: current value, previous year’s value, absolute and percentage change. The year-on-year comparison logic has to stay correct with any month filter active, which calls for time intelligence measures built properly from the model up.

What I learnt

The disconnected table for the month selector was the technical decision with the biggest impact on the model’s reliability. Without it, filtering by a specific month made the year-on-year measures return wrong results, because the filter affected the current year and the previous year at the same time. I also learnt that nine KPIs on a single page only work if the design has a clear hierarchy. Without it, the density of information paralyses the user instead of guiding them.

678.208€
Free cash flow 2023
-1.165.649€
Free cash flow previous year

Free cash flow went from −€1.17m to €678,208 in one year — the model shows exactly which items explain the difference.

Accounting star schemaFinancial DAXBalance sheet · P&L · Cash flowEBITDAVertical and horizontal analysisVariance against budgetYear-end projectionDrill-through to journal entryFinancial analytics

The problem

When each area analyses its own part separately, leadership has to build the full picture by hand every time. That process is slow, hard to trace and produces different versions of the same reality. Without a model that connected the three financial statements consistently, any analysis was a partial snapshot.

The goal

To design an accounting structure that brought together the balance sheet, P&L and cash flow, stayed consistent across periods and let you navigate from a high-level KPI down to the exact journal entry behind it, without leaving the report.

My approach

I started with the model. Two fact tables: FactDiario with the actual journal entries and FactPresupuesto with the budget data, connected to the same Calendar, Companies, Sources, Accounts and Partners dimensions. Four extra dimensions for the accounting hierarchy: dimPyG, dimBalance, dimCF and DimApuntes, each with its own structure of levels.

A TablaFechaCierre table handles the year-end projection: it extrapolates the expected result at year end from the actual data available up to the cut-off date, while staying consistent with the original budget.

The report has six areas with navigation: a KPI panel with EBITDA, ROE, ROA, acid test, solvency and free cash flow; a validation checklist that catches unbalanced entries and unclassified accounts; a trial balance with drill-through to the individual entry; a balance sheet with vertical and horizontal analysis and trend over time; a P&L with three views (quarterly trend, comparison against budget and year-end projection); and cash flow with operating, investing and financing flows compared with the previous year.

Key findings

The validation checklist was one of the most important design decisions. In a model that consolidates several companies with different data sources, having a page dedicated to catching unbalanced entries and unclassified 4-digit accounts before moving on to the analysis stops an accounting error from reaching a leadership meeting as if it were a real result. In 2023, free cash flow was €678,208, against −€1,165,649 the year before. The model shows exactly which items explain that difference.

Tools used

Power BI for the complete model, navigation between the report’s six areas and drill-through from any KPI down to the source journal entry. DAX for all the financial measures: solvency and liquidity ratios, EBITDA calculations, year-on-year change, variance against budget and the year-end projection logic, which combines actual data up to the cut-off date with an extrapolation of the rest of the year.

What I learnt

Consolidating several companies into a single model adds a layer of complexity that doesn’t exist in single-company projects. The relationships between FactDiario and the dimensions have to be designed so that filtering by company doesn’t break the consistency of the consolidated totals. The year-end projection was the most demanding technical piece: to work, it has to tell months with actual data apart from months without it, and the extrapolation logic has to be consistent both in the table and in the trend chart.

P&L in Power BIFinancial DAXSmart subtotalsActual vs budgetWaterfall chartAccounting sign controlStar schema modellingFinancial analyticsAccounting hierarchy

The problem

When financial data reaches BI without an accounting structure behind it, the problems are predictable: totals that don’t add up when you change the filter, signs that flip depending on the context, subtotals that aggregate what they shouldn’t. In a report leadership uses to make decisions, losing consistency means losing credibility.

The goal

To build a P&L that works in any scenario: with cross-filters by year, quarter, department and organisation, with actual vs budget comparable in the same report, and with subtotals that always add up without relying on visual patches.

My approach

I built the model from the accounting logic, not from the visual. Every account is explicitly classified as revenue, cost or expense, with sign rules defined at model level so the totals add up at any filter level. The subtotals — Gross margin, Operating profit and Net profit — each follow their own accounting structure, not an automatic aggregation.

The model sits on a central fact table, PyG, with six dimensions: Calendar, Accounts, Departments, Scenario, Organisations and Headers. The measures are organised into six folders: Basic, Amount, Budget, Actual, Variance and Time. On that base I built the actual vs budget comparison with absolute change, percentage variance and a waterfall chart that doesn’t just show the difference but explains which items create it and in which direction.

Key findings

The dashboard has two views. The first is the expandable P&L with four years of data (2020–2023), actual and budget in parallel columns, with visual variance indicators for each line. The second is the detail view with the complete comparative income statement plus two waterfall charts. In 2021, actual net profit was €6.78m against a budget of €3.53m, a positive variance of 92.1%. The waterfall shows the main driver was revenue growth, not cost control.

Tools used

Power BI for the complete model, the report’s two views and navigation between them. DAX for the subtotals with accounting logic, sign control, the actual vs budget variance measures and the waterfall calculations. The Beneficio neto cascada measure is the most complex piece of the model: it has to stay consistent both in the table and in the waterfall chart with any combination of active filters.

What I learnt

The most demanding part wasn’t building the visual but making sure the accounting signs were right at all times. Revenue and costs carry different accounting signs that don’t match how Power BI aggregates by default. Without that logic solved in the model, the waterfall chart showed the right numbers going in the wrong direction. I also learnt that splitting measures into themed folders from the start isn’t an aesthetic preference: it’s a decision that saves hours as the model grows.

The same price can look expensive or cheap depending on the context — 75% of hosts have a single property, but 31 professional operators concentrate supply in the areas with the highest demand.

Territorial analysisMaps in Power BIMarket segmentationKPIsExploratory analysisDAX

The problem

Most tourism market analyses work with overall averages. An average that mixes San Sebastián in August with an inland town in January, an entire home with a private room, an occasional host with one who manages thirty properties. The same price can look expensive or cheap depending on the context. Without structure, data doesn’t inform.

The goal

To build an analysis that answers a more useful question than "is it expensive?": whether a high price reflects high demand, low supply, the professionalisation of the market or one-off seasonality. And to do it starting from the dimension that usual analyses ignore: territory.

My approach

The tourism market isn’t uniform: it’s spatial before it’s statistical. I designed the model around that premise: the map as the main axis of reading, with filters that change the spatial pattern in real time by province, type of accommodation, price range and minimum availability.

The model sits on a star schema with fact_Listings at the centre and four dimensions: Location, Room type, Host and Calendar. An extra price segmentation dimension classifies each property into bands: Budget, Mid-range, High and Luxury. On that base I built the segmentation that usual analyses leave out: price by type of accommodation and the distinction between occasional hosts and professional operators with a portfolio.

Key findings

75% of hosts are occasional, with a single property. Professional operators with more than 15 properties number only 31, but they concentrate a disproportionate share of supply in high-demand areas. Bilbao and San Sebastián have the highest volume of monthly reviews, with 428 and 391 respectively. Entire homes have an average price of €211.78, more than double that of a private room (€95.24). The difference isn’t just the type: they’re different buyers, different areas and different seasonality.

Tools used

Power BI for the data model, the maps with each property geolocated and the full dashboard with navigation between the analysis view and the map view. DAX for the average price, availability and segment classification measures, and the logic that classifies hosts by portfolio size.

What I learnt

Working with geospatial data in Power BI is more complex than it looks: the quality of the map depends directly on the quality of the coordinates and on how the location dimension is modelled. I also learnt that segmenting price into bands is as much a design decision as a technical one: the cut-offs have to make sense for the specific market you’re analysing, not be generic percentiles.

111 Loyal customers generated almost half the revenue and hadn’t bought anything for months — €198,000 at risk that, without segmentation, had neither a name nor a number.

RFM Customer segmentation Advanced DAX Star schema HTML table in DAX Conditional KPIs Dynamic percentiles B2B analytics Customer analytics

The problem

The sales director had the numbers but couldn’t make sense of them. Revenue falling, customer base growing. The first reaction was to look for more sales. But before that we had to confirm whether the problem was in acquisition or retention. Without a way to see how each customer actually behaves, that question has no answer.

The goal

To classify the 400 customers by how they really buy — when they last bought, how often and how much — to find which segments were losing activity and how much revenue was genuinely at risk.

My approach

I built an RFM model on the order history. Each customer gets three scores: Recency (days since the last purchase), Frequency (number of orders) and Monetary (total amount spent). The cut-offs aren’t fixed: they’re calculated with dynamic percentiles over the whole table, so the model works with any data distribution without having to adjust thresholds by hand. With those three scores, each customer is assigned to one of seven segments: Champion, Loyal, Potential, At Risk, Needs Attention, Average and Lost.

The model sits on a star schema with Fact_Pedidos at the centre and five dimensions: Customer, Date, Product, Sales rep and Venue type. All the RFM columns, including the three scores and the final segment, live in Dim_Cliente. The dashboard answers four questions on a single page: business health, split by segment, monthly revenue trend and the recommended sales action for each group — the last one generated as an HTML table straight from a DAX measure.

Key findings

The Loyal segment held 111 customers and almost half of total revenue. Their recency had been growing for months. More than €198,000 in customers who used to buy well and had not been seen for a while. Without segmentation, that signal gets lost in the total. With it, it has a name, a figure and a specific action: reactivate now. Champions were only 34 customers, with an average recency of 14 days and an average order of €4,787. The most active and the most profitable, getting the same treatment as everyone else.

Tools used

Power BI to model the data, calculate RFM in DAX and build the full dashboard with filters by area, venue, sales rep and year. DAX for the three scores, the segment assignment and the sales action table. The final table is generated as HTML inside a measure, with no need for external visuals.

What I learnt

The technical side wasn’t the hardest part. The hard part was deciding the segmentation cut-offs: when a customer moves from Loyal to At Risk can’t be an arbitrary number, it has to reflect how the business really behaves. Using dynamic percentiles instead of fixed thresholds was the decision that most improved the model’s quality. Generating an HTML table from DAX also has its limitations in Power BI, and those limitations force you to think harder about the design before writing the first line of code.
