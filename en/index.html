Sales analysis · IBCS
Sales report with an IBCS approach
3
IBCS visual types built
5
Data model dimensions

Two periods can close with the same total and have been completely different underneath — without the change built into the visual, you can’t see that difference.

IBCS DAX Visual calculations Star schema modelling Absolute and relative change Sales analytics YoY analysis

The problem

In many sales teams, sales analysis starts and ends with the total. If the figure goes up, all is well. The problem is that two periods can close with the same number and have been completely different underneath: one with real growth, the other with a fall offset by a one-off campaign. Without the change built into the visual, you can’t see that difference.

The goal

To build a sales report that lets you read volume and change in a single view, without switching tabs or comparing separate tables. And to do it following the IBCS standard, which defines how to show that information so that everyone reads it the same way, without ambiguity.

My approach

The IBCS (International Business Communication Standards) standard sets concrete rules for showing change: which colour means a positive variance, which shape means actual versus budget, and how volume and change share the same axis without overlapping or getting mixed up.

I built three types of visual following that standard. The multi-tier column chart shows the monthly absolute value, the absolute change against last year and the percentage change at the same time. The column chart with built-in change packs that information into each bar, which helps when the axis has many items. The variance line chart shows the trend over time, with the area between the actual line and the target shaded according to whether the target is met.

The technical key is combining DAX with visual calculations: DAX for the model’s core logic and the dynamic measure selector, and visual calculations to generate the changes directly on the visual without multiplying measures in the model.

Tools used

Power BI for the model, the three IBCS visual types and navigation between views. DAX and visual calculations together for the CY vs SPLY and against-target changes.

What I learnt

Replicating the IBCS standard in Power BI without custom visuals forces you to understand how measure-level conditional formatting works. The hardest part was keeping the three tiers of the multi-tier column chart in the right proportions whatever the value range of the selected filter.

Financial analysis
Complete financial report
€678,208
Free cash flow 2023
−€1,165,649
Free cash flow previous year

Free cash flow went from −€1.17m to €678,208 in one year — the model shows exactly which items explain the difference.

Accounting star schema Financial DAX Balance sheet · P&L · Cash flow EBITDA Year-end projection Drill-through to journal entry Financial analytics

The problem

When each area analyses its own part separately, leadership has to build the full picture by hand every time. That process is slow, hard to trace and produces different versions of the same reality. Without a model that connected the three financial statements consistently, any analysis was a partial snapshot.

The goal

To design an accounting structure that brought together the balance sheet, P&L and cash flow, stayed consistent across periods and let you navigate from a high-level KPI down to the exact journal entry behind it, without leaving the report.

My approach

I started with the model. Two fact tables: FactDiario with the actual accounting entries and FactPresupuesto with the budget data, connected to the same dimensions. A TablaFechaCierre table handles the year-end projection: it extrapolates the expected year-end result from the actual data available up to the cut-off date.

The report has six areas: a KPI panel with EBITDA, ROE, ROA, acid test, solvency and free cash flow; a validation checklist that catches unbalanced entries and unclassified accounts; a trial balance with drill-through to the individual entry; a balance sheet with vertical and horizontal analysis; a P&L with three views; and cash flow with the three types of flow compared with the previous year.

Key findings

The validation checklist was one of the most important decisions. In a model that consolidates several companies, a page dedicated to catching unbalanced entries and unclassified accounts stops an accounting error from reaching a board meeting as if it were a real result. In 2023, free cash flow was €678,208 against −€1,165,649 the year before.

Tools used

Power BI for the full model, navigation between the six areas and drill-through to the source accounting entry. DAX for solvency and liquidity ratios, EBITDA, year-on-year change, variance against budget and the year-end projection logic.

What I learnt

Consolidating several companies in a single model adds a layer of complexity that single-company projects don’t have. The year-end projection was the hardest technical piece: it has to tell months with actual data apart from months without it, and stay consistent in both the table and the trend chart.

RFM analysis — B2B hospitality distributor

111 Loyal customers generated almost half the revenue and hadn’t bought anything for months — €198,000 at risk that, without segmentation, had neither a name nor a number.

RFM Customer segmentation Advanced DAX Star schema HTML table in DAX Dynamic percentiles B2B analytics Customer analytics

The problem

The sales director had the numbers but couldn’t make sense of them. Revenue falling, customer base growing. The first reaction was to look for more sales. But first we had to confirm whether the problem was in acquisition or retention. Without a way to see how each customer actually behaves, that question has no answer.

The goal

To classify the 400 customers by how they really buy — when they last bought, how often and how much — to find which segments were losing activity and how much revenue was genuinely at risk.

My approach

I built an RFM model on the order history. Each customer gets three scores: Recency (days since the last purchase), Frequency (number of orders) and Monetary (total amount spent). The cut-offs are calculated with dynamic percentiles across the whole table, so the model works well with any data distribution.

The model sits on a star schema with Fact_Pedidos at the centre and five dimensions: Customer, Date, Product, Sales rep and Venue type. The dashboard answers four questions on a single page: business health, split by segment, monthly revenue trend and the recommended sales action for each group — the last one generated as an HTML table straight from a DAX measure.

Key findings

The Loyal segment held 111 customers and almost half of total revenue. Their recency had been growing for months. More than €198,000 in customers who used to buy well and had not been seen for a while. Without segmentation, that signal gets lost in the total. Champions were only 34 customers, with an average recency of 14 days and an average order of €4,787, and they were treated the same as everyone else.

Tools used

Power BI for the model and the full dashboard, with filters by area, venue, sales rep and year. DAX for the three scores, the segment assignment and the sales action table, generated as HTML inside a measure.

What I learnt

The tricky part was deciding the segmentation cut-offs: when a customer moves from Loyal to At Risk can’t be an arbitrary number. Using dynamic percentiles instead of fixed thresholds was the decision that improved the model most. Generating an HTML table from DAX forces you to think harder about the design before writing the first line of code.

Kosta Cálida — Reporting for a store network

+37.58%
Margin · Drinks and soft drinks

Mahon was the store with the highest sales (€277,000), but not the most profitable — margin by category told a story the total was hiding.

Star schema modelling Two fact tables Disconnected T_DISC tables Dynamic TopN Sales vs targets Drill-down to receipt YTD Retail analytics

The problem

A store chain with complete data in its tills but no way to answer basic business questions: which store performs best, where margin is being lost, whether targets are met or it’s just beating last month. The data existed; the problem was that nobody could read it together.

The goal

To design a complete reporting system — from the data model to the dashboard — that turned scattered numbers into concrete decisions. With a single report, leadership had to be able to see sales, profitability and target attainment for each store in one read.

My approach

The model has two fact tables: Ventas with the actual till data and Presupuestos with the targets by store and period. Both connect to the same dimensions through disconnected T_DISC tables that let you change the axis of analysis without affecting the model’s relationships. The TopN parameters filter the most relevant stores or products dynamically.

The report covers four angles: an executive summary with sales, margin and attainment KPIs; store analysis with a ranking and comparison against target; product analysis with average order, units and profitability; and trends over time with YTD and year-on-year comparison.

Key findings

Reading sales and margin together revealed high-volume, low-profitability stores that weren’t visible in the previous reports. The drill-down to the individual receipt identified the exact product that explained the variance in a specific store, something that used to mean exporting to Excel and cross-referencing by hand.

Tools used

Power BI for the full model and the dashboard, with navigation between the four areas. DAX for attainment against budget, YTD, year-on-year change, margin by product and dynamic ranking logic with TopN parameters. Power Query to bring together the till sources and the targets.

What I learnt

Disconnected T_DISC tables are essential when the same selector has to affect two fact tables without creating ambiguity in the model. Designing the model with that separation from the start avoids having to rebuild the relationships when the business grows and adds new stores or product lines.
